AI GovernanceFor companies that use, build or buy AI.

For companies that use AI

Use AI across your business, with the documents the EU AI Act requires.

Your teams may already use AI in hiring, customer service or decisions about people. The EU AI Act places duties on the company that uses an AI system as well as on the company that built it. The heaviest duties, for high-risk uses (uses that can significantly affect people), apply from December 2027. Boards, auditors and customers already ask about AI use.

Provenance lists the AI you use, gathers the facts your lawyers need to classify each AI system under the EU AI Act, and prepares the assessments, policies and records that the Act may require. You receive documents that your own people can keep up to date.

What you get

  • The facts your lawyers need to classify each system under the EU AI Act
  • One combined assessment of data protection and of effects on fundamental rights, where the law requires both
  • Internal AI policies, short AI literacy training for your staff, and a 90-day plan that names an owner for each action
Planned length
Two to four weeks. A shorter version is planned to take about two weeks.

For teams building a product

Launch your AI product without having to rebuild it later for EU legal provisions.

A new AI or data product may need to satisfy several things at once: the EU AI Act, the GDPR, the Data Act, the EU legal provisions on sending data outside the EU, and whatever your first customers write into their contracts. Each one affects the others. If different people handle them one at a time, the answers can conflict. Changing a product after launch can cost more time and money than planning for EU legal provisions from the start.

Provenance reviews all of these together, early in the project, and turns the results into decisions your team can act on: what to build in, what to document and what to leave out. The heaviest EU AI Act duties, for high-risk systems, start to apply from December 2027. Customers, boards and purchasing teams already ask about these.

For companies buying AI

Buy the AI tool knowing what you are getting.

A demo shows what an AI tool can do. A demo does not show where your data goes, whether the vendor uses it to train its model, or what the EU AI Act may require of you once you start using the tool. The company that uses an AI system can have duties of its own.

Provenance asks each vendor on your shortlist the same questions, checks the answers against the vendor’s own documents, and gives you a scorecard that compares the vendors point by point. You choose based on facts, and your lawyers negotiate based on the same facts.

For companies that sell

Answer the questionnaire that is delaying the signing of your contract and your first invoice.

Your customer has chosen your product and the price is agreed. Then the customer’s purchasing team sends a privacy, security or AI questionnaire, and signing waits for your answers. When a customer questionnaire delays signing, it can also delay your first invoice, sometimes past the end of a quarter.

Provenance prepares the answers with you, or checks the answers you have already drafted. Each answer is tied to a document, record or number from your own business, so the customer’s reviewers can check it. Your engineers keep working on the product: Provenance asks them short questions in terms they use and prepares the rest with you. You keep the finished answers and the documents behind them, so when your next customer asks the same questions, you already have the answers.

No one can promise a signature or a date, because the decision belongs to your customer. Provenance aims to reduce avoidable waiting caused by unclear answers, missing evidence and gaps that nobody mentioned until the buyer found them. Provenance sets out the facts, and your lawyers write the contract terms. Provenance never acts for both sides of the same deal.

For suppliers to banks and insurers

Sell to banks and insurers: comply with the DORA provisions they require from their suppliers.

If you supply software or services to banks, insurers or other operators of essential services in Europe, their duties under DORA and NIS2 (EU laws on digital resilience and network security) can reach you through the contract, often as a long list of operational commitments that your delivery team may not have reviewed.

Provenance compares each requirement with what you can show today and marks each gap it finds. Estimating the cost of closing a gap, and closing it, are separate pieces of work: you can ask Provenance for a written scope and a fixed fee for either. Your lawyers negotiate based on facts.

For companies whose customers ask where their data goes

Show customers where their data is stored and where it is sent.

If you send personal data outside the EU, for example to a US cloud service or a support team in India, EU data protection law requires safeguards for the transfer, and you may need to show them. Customers and authorities now ask to see the documents, not only to hear that the documents exist.

Provenance maps where your data comes from and where it goes, and collects the documented facts behind each transfer. Whether a transfer is lawful remains a question for your lawyers, who will have the file to answer it.

Know which data you can ask for, and which data you must share.

The EU Data Act changes who may use the data that connected products and cloud services generate. For many businesses, the Act brings new rights and new duties, and these are easy to miss until a business partner asks about them.

Provenance works out what the Act means for your products and your contracts, and explains clearly what you can ask for and what you must provide.

Month by month

Ongoing privacy and AI support, without a full-time hire.

An assessment is accurate on the day it is finished. After that, the product changes, a new tool arrives or a law is amended. Many growing companies need an experienced person to follow these changes, but not forty hours a week.

Provenance can work with you in two ways. One way is as your designated data protection officer (DPO): the formal GDPR role, held under a service contract, with the independence the law requires. The other way is as privacy support: the same day-to-day work, for a company that is not required to appoint a DPO.

The law makes a DPO mandatory only for certain organisations. A first conversation settles which arrangement fits, and if you do not need a formal DPO, Provenance will tell you plainly.

For companies based outside the EU

Your EU representative: a named person, not a new office.

If your company has no establishment in the EU but offers goods or services to people there, or monitors their behaviour, Article 27 of the GDPR generally requires a named representative inside the Union. Customers and tenders ask for that name early.

You do not need a subsidiary or an employee to meet it. The representative is appointed in writing, under a service contract. Once Provenance is established in Portugal, it can be that representative: the contact for individuals and authorities, with the record of processing kept alongside. You get a senior person who also understands your systems.

Check that it applies to you. Not every company needs one. The duty does not apply where processing is occasional, involves no large-scale sensitive data and is unlikely to put people at risk. It does not apply to public bodies. The representative also has to be in an EU country where some of the people concerned are. Provenance checks these points with you first.

A representative is not a data protection officer and not your legal adviser, and your own responsibility under the GDPR stays with you. Provenance never holds both roles for one client. A similar role exists under the EU AI Act for some AI providers outside the Union. Ask if that may be you.

  • A clear route for requests from individuals and authorities
